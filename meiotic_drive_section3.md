## Meiotic Drive on the Sex Chromosomes

Mammalian X and Y chromosomes do not recombine along most of their length, so a gene on the X is never paired with one on the Y in the next generation. This makes them evolutionary rivals. Any X-linked gene that impairs Y-bearing sperm increases its own transmission and spreads. This is meiotic drive. A driver need only break a shared cellular component differentially, so no novel function is required.

The transmission advantage of a driver easily outweighs moderate organismal harm. A gene's net transmission equals its organismal fitness multiplied by its segregation ratio, so a driver that reduces fitness by 5% but raises its segregation to 60% transmits at 0.95 × 0.60 = 0.57, compared with 1.00 × 0.50 = 0.50 for the non-driving allele at the same locus. The selection coefficient is s = (0.57 − 0.50)/0.50 = 0.14, exceeding the ~10% attributed to lactase persistence — the strongest positive selection documented in humans. Moreover, the fitness cost of a driver may often not reduce its segregation advantage at all. If a driver impairs a neurodevelopmental gene, the organism's probability of survival to reproduction is lowered, but the transmission distortion in sperm is unaffected. In such cases the full segregation advantage applies. Even a 10% reduction in survival to reproduction yields s = (0.90 × 0.60 − 0.50)/0.50 = 0.08, so the driver still spreads under selection comparable to lactase persistence despite causing substantial organismal harm. A rapidly spreading driver drags along all physically linked variants in what is called a selective sweep. This hitchhiking elevates nearby alleles indiscriminately, including deleterious ones.

An even sex ratio is a Nash equilibrium (Fisher 1930). When one sex becomes rarer it is reproductively more valuable, so any variant anywhere in the genome that restores balanced transmission is immediately favoured. Selection on such suppressors is proportional to the sex-ratio deviation and comparable in strength to selection on the driver itself. Suppressors therefore undergo their own sweeps, hitchhiking their own deleterious neighbours to high frequency. Each cycle of driver, suppressor, and new driver deposits collateral genetic damage on both sides.
